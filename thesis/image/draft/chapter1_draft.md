# Chapter 1

## The Problem and Its Background

### Introduction

This chapter gives the background of the study, the statement of the problem, the objectives, the significance, and the scope and limitations. The central concern is how well a generative AI pipeline can produce a patient face that shows the clinical signs described in a nursing scenario, and whether a diffusion-generated image or a parametric three-dimensional head rendering does it more faithfully.

---

### Background of the Study

Philippine nursing programs must train students to handle clinical problems safely, and the Commission on Higher Education requires outcome-based teaching to do it (Commission on Higher Education, 2017). Simulation carries much of that training because students can practice drug administration and emergency response without putting a real patient at risk (Bambini, 2016; Jeffries, 2012). This training needs faculty, and nursing schools do not have enough of them. In one urban area of the Central Philippines, most clinical instructors were part-time and had only a few years of service (Noblezada et al., 2025), and many experienced nurses keep leaving for jobs abroad (Alibudbud, 2023). The shortage is not only local. American nursing schools reported a 7.2 percent vacancy rate in full-time faculty posts and turned away more than 92,000 qualified applicants in a single year because they could not find enough teachers (American Association of Colleges of Nursing, 2025).

Faculty also lose many hours writing the scenarios themselves. A complete scenario needs a patient history, changing vital signs, lab and imaging results, doctor's orders, medication records, and notes for the facilitator, and the writing process is time intensive (Bambini, 2016). The Healthcare Simulation Standards of Best Practice add more requirements, such as clear objectives, realistic detail, and a planned debriefing (Watts et al., 2021). Teachers who tried AI for the writing step said the tools produced a usable draft quickly, but that important details were often missing and someone still had to check the content before it was safe to use (Harder, 2023; Vaughn & Ford, 2024). Even when the scenario text is accurate, though, it leaves one thing missing. The student reads about a patient with jaundice, pallor, or ptosis, but cannot see that patient. Physical signs are visual; text describes them, but a description is not the same as seeing them, and that gap is what the student has to close through imagination every time.

Generative AI can produce faces from text, but the available evidence says that plain text-to-image models fail at this task when the signs are clinical. Kumar et al. (2024) tested DALL-E 2 and Midjourney on medical education illustrations for hypothyroidism and Horner syndrome. For hypothyroidism, whose signs are symmetric and generalized (facial puffiness, periorbital edema, and coarse features), the models performed relatively well. For Horner syndrome, which produces unilateral ptosis and anisocoria on one side of the face, both tools consistently failed to render the asymmetric signs accurately, producing symmetry-biased or anatomically distorted outputs even with repeated prompt refinements. Fan et al. (2023) generated faces with pathological features for medical education using DALL-E-powered tools and found that the approach showed potential for bypassing patient consent barriers, but cautioned that images require expert review before formal curriculum use. Haider et al. (2025) evaluated four text-to-image models (Midjourney, DALL-E 3, Gemini Ultra, Stable Diffusion) on craniofacial anatomy and found high inter-rater reliability among expert reviewers (ICC = 0.858), but all tested models produced critical structural flaws, including missing foramina, incorrect suture lines, and misconfigured neurovascular pathways. A 2025 evaluation of DALL-E 3 for pediatric ophthalmology educational images similarly found poor accuracy for clinical features, reporting a median pathological accuracy score of only 1 out of 15 (Jong et al., 2025). The pattern across these studies is consistent: these models can generate a face that looks like a person, but they cannot reliably render a specific, documented clinical sign on that face, especially when the sign is asymmetric or requires precise regional anatomy.

The failure is not only about sign accuracy. Skin-tone representation is a documented problem that matters for nursing education in a country as demographically diverse as the Philippines. Joerg et al. (2025) analyzed 4,000 dermatology images generated by leading text-to-image models and found that 89.8 percent depicted light skin tones. When blinded dermatology residents reviewed the same images for clinical accuracy, only 15 percent were correctly identified as accurately depicting the intended skin condition. O'Malley et al. (2024) showed that injecting an explicit demographic description into the prompt substantially shifted the skin-tone distribution toward the intended population (P = .04 for the corrected distribution against expected demographics), but the correction required deliberate engineering and did not happen by default with standard prompts. Most studies on AI-generated clinical images test one model on one condition, usually dermatology or ophthalmology, and few look at whether the rendering method itself changes sign accuracy. No study found in the literature compared two rendering modalities, diffusion-generated photographs versus a parametric three-dimensional head, for the same clinical signs, using the same pipeline, with nursing faculty as raters, inside a working school hospital information system. That is the gap this study fills.

---

### Statement of the Problem

Nursing scenario descriptions give students the text of a patient's condition, but not a face. Physical signs such as jaundice, ptosis, facial droop, cyanosis, and pallor are visual; students must imagine them from prose, which introduces the risk of forming an inaccurate or incomplete mental image before they ever see the real sign in clinic. Generative AI can produce faces from text, but plain text-to-image models are documented to fail on clinical facial signs, especially asymmetric ones. Both diffusion-generated photographs and parametric three-dimensional head models can, in principle, render signs from a structured description, but they work in different ways and have different failure modes. No study in the literature has run both under the same pipeline with the same evaluation criteria and had clinical educators judge the results.

This study therefore builds a scenario-to-face generation pipeline integrated into Chansey, an educational hospital information system, and compares diffusion-generated images against parametric 3D head outputs under a single constant agentic pipeline. It answers the following questions:

1. What is the per-trait fidelity of diffusion-generated PNG versus parametric 3D generated patient faces, measured as trait recall rate and number of hallucinated extra signs, against a clinician-authored trait checklist?
2. What is the clinical plausibility and teaching usefulness of each rendering modality, as rated by Nursing Department faculty?
3. Is there a significant difference between the two modalities on trait fidelity, faculty-rated plausibility, and skin-tone fidelity?
4. What is the latency, token cost, and GPU requirement of each modality under the same pipeline?
5. Which modality is more suitable for patient face visualization in Chansey, and why?

---

### Objectives of the Study

This study develops and evaluates a patient-face generation pipeline embedded in the Faculty Scenario Builder of Chansey, an educational hospital information system. The pipeline parses a scenario description into a structured trait schema, renders two versions of the patient face through different modalities, verifies each output, and routes it for faculty approval. Specifically, this study aims to:

1. Design and build the scenario-to-face generation pipeline as a Python microservice that the Laravel Chansey application calls through an HTTP interface, including a parser, an expander for disease-only inputs, a diffusion renderer, a parametric 3D renderer, and a VLM-based verifier.
2. Evaluate the per-trait fidelity of each modality, measuring trait recall rate and the count of hallucinated extra signs against a clinician-authored checklist, and assess faculty-rated clinical plausibility and teaching usefulness (RQ1, RQ2).
3. Determine whether a statistically significant difference exists between the two modalities on trait fidelity, clinical plausibility, and skin-tone fidelity (RQ3).
4. Measure the latency, token cost, and GPU requirement of each modality under the same pipeline for realistic classroom deployment assessment (RQ4).
5. Recommend the more suitable modality for patient face visualization in Chansey, supported by the evidence from objectives 2 through 4 (RQ5).

---

### Significance of the Study

This study examines whether a generative pipeline that runs two different rendering modalities can produce a reliable visual of the patient described in a nursing scenario.

It will help Nursing Department faculty by giving them a tested face generation tool inside the scenario builder they already use. A faculty member can type a patient description or a diagnosis and receive a rendered patient face for that scenario without sourcing copyrighted textbook images or using real patient photographs. The tool keeps every generated face under faculty approval before it attaches to a scenario, so clinical control stays with the instructor.

It will help nursing students by closing the gap between a written scenario and the physical appearance of the condition it describes. When students see a rendered face showing ptosis, jaundice, or cyanosis next to the scenario text, they practice matching visual signs to clinical language before they encounter those signs in a real patient. The study also measures whether either rendering modality represents darker skin tones accurately, because students in the Philippines need to recognize signs across diverse skin presentations.

It will help developers and nursing informatics researchers by providing measured evidence on two rendering approaches applied to clinical facial signs, an area with few faculty-rated results inside a working school system. The trait schema, rubric, verifier design, and audit trails the pipeline produces are reusable for future work, including fine-tuned models once the system has accumulated a corpus of instructor-approved images.

It will help schools and training programs by demonstrating a path to patient face generation that does not require real patient photographs, does not need a GPU on site, and keeps every output under faculty review before any student sees it. That design supports the outcome-based teaching standards that CHED Memorandum Order No. 15 requires of nursing programs (Commission on Higher Education, 2017).

It will help future researchers by leaving a documented pipeline, a pre-registered test set of scenario descriptions, an evaluation rubric, and an audit dataset that can be extended to more signs, other body regions, larger rater pools, or a fine-tuned renderer trained on approved images.

---

### Scope and Limitations

This study covers the design, development, and evaluation of a patient-face generation pipeline integrated into the Faculty Scenario Builder of Chansey, an educational hospital information system. The pipeline generates faces from faculty-authored scenario descriptions and operates through a Python microservice that Chansey calls over HTTP. Both rendering modalities use the same agentic pipeline: a large language model parses the input into a structured trait schema, an expander handles disease-only inputs by proposing visible signs for instructor confirmation, a renderer produces the output for each modality, and a VLM-based verifier checks each trait against the rendered image before the face reaches faculty approval.

The study compares two rendering modalities. Arm A is diffusion-based image generation, which runs through a commercial image API and produces a photograph-style PNG. Arm B is parametric 3D head generation, which uses FLAME 2023 Open (CC-BY-4.0) to produce a stylized head rendered to a static profile PNG. Both modalities use the same commercial large language model through an API. The study uses models through APIs and does not train or fine-tune any model; training is future work contingent on accumulating a corpus of instructor-approved images. The experimental stimuli are a pre-registered set of 24 to 30 scenario descriptions covering color signs (jaundice, pallor, cyanosis, flushing), structural and asymmetric signs (ptosis, facial droop, periorbital edema), and secretions (rhinorrhea), across both sexes and the full Monk/Fitzpatrick skin-tone range. Nursing Department faculty rate the generated faces on a rubric covering trait accuracy, clinical plausibility, and teaching usefulness. The face scope covers the head only; full-body generation is out of scope.

The study has limits. The test set is small, so results come from one school and may not generalize to other programs or sign catalogs. All faces are synthetic; no real patient photographs are used at any stage. The raters are Nursing Department faculty, not licensed physicians, so the study does not claim physician-level diagnostic accuracy. The diffusion arm runs on a hosted API, and a change in the provider's model version or pricing can affect repeat results over time. The parametric 3D arm contains a planned go/no-go spike for unilateral blendshapes (ptosis, facial droop); if that spike fails, the arm's sign set reshuffles to color and bilateral structural signs, and the study continues with the remaining signs on both arms. The study does not cover autonomous publishing; generated faces stay as unapproved previews until faculty approve them and attach them to a scenario.
